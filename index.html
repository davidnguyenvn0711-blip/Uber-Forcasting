01 —

The Business
Problem

Uber does not own a single car. Its entire business is a real-time matching problem: at any given moment, how many people want a ride, and are enough drivers in the right places to give them one? Get that balance right and rides are fast, prices stay reasonable, and drivers earn steadily. Get it wrong — too few drivers when a concert lets out, a stadium empties, or a rainstorm hits — and prices spike to punishing levels, wait times balloon, riders open Lyft, and the revenue that was supposed to be there evaporates in minutes. Demand forecasting is the engine underneath all of it. Before every Friday night, every holiday, every major event, Uber's system generates a prediction: how many trips will be requested, in which neighborhoods, in which hour. That number flows directly into driver incentive algorithms, surge pricing thresholds, and server provisioning decisions — automatically, with no human in the loop. A 10% forecast error is not a rounding mistake. It is the difference between a smooth night and a crisis that no one sees coming until riders are already angry and drivers have already gone home.

02 —

The Data
& the Model

I built two years of historical Friday night Uber trip data near WWU's campus in Bellingham — 104 weeks spanning January 2023 through December 2024 — and layered in four real-world demand signals: a long-run upward trend as Uber's Bellingham market grew, a summer seasonality bump of roughly 20%, a school-year effect that added 15% ridership when WWU was in session, and a New Year's Eve spike that tripled normal volume. Before touching any model, I spent time in exploratory analysis: plotting the full series, annotating summer starts, and identifying the NYE outlier — because a model trained on data you haven't looked at is a model you cannot explain or defend. I then engineered those signals into numeric features, split the data chronologically (78 weeks training, 26 weeks holdout), trained a linear regression model, and evaluated it against weeks it had genuinely never seen.

Step 1
Data Simulation & Exploration
Built 104 weeks of synthetic Friday night trip data with realistic trend, seasonality, school-year effect, and NYE spike. Plotted and explored the full series before modeling anything.
Step 2
Feature Engineering
Translated calendar dates into numeric signals: week_num (trend clock), is_summer, is_nye, is_school_year. Each feature answers a specific business question the model needs to weigh.
Step 2
Chronological Train / Test Split
Weeks 1–78 for training, weeks 79–104 as a held-out backtest. Never random — time-series data must be split in time order to prevent the model from seeing the future during training.
Step 2
Linear Regression & Evaluation
Trained a linear regression model on the four features. Achieved a test-set MAE of 12 trips — acceptable for operational planning on a Bellingham Friday night.

04 —

Stress-Testing
the Forecast

Before sending that memo, I stress-tested the key assumptions to understand how fragile the recommendation was.

Stress-Test Result

There was no flip row in the table because surge never turned off in any scenario I tested. Even when I increased driver availability and lowered demand to the conservative bound, the system still showed a shortage — so surge stayed on the entire time.

The assumption that had the biggest impact was trips per driver per hour, not driver availability. When I lowered it toward 1.5 to reflect post-concert congestion, the number of drivers needed jumped sharply because each driver could complete fewer trips in the rush window. In contrast, increasing driver availability did not change the outcome much because the demand gap was still too large to close.

The key conclusion: the surge call is very stable, not fragile. It does not flip easily across scenarios. The real sensitivity is driver throughput — if that drops due to traffic or slow pickups near the venue, the shortage becomes significantly worse and reinforces the need for surge rather than removing it.

What stress-testing reveals
Robustness vs. Fragility
When the driver number barely moves across scenarios, you can send the memo with confidence. When it swings by 50% because one assumption shifts, your boss needs to know that before acting — the decision is only as good as the uncertainty they understand.
Standard analyst practice
Isolate One Variable
Changing one assumption at a time reveals which lever actually drives the outcome. Change everything simultaneously and you cannot explain what caused the shift to a stakeholder — or to yourself. It is the same logic as a controlled experiment.
05 —

When the
Algorithm Decides

The moment a predicted trip count exists, it flows directly into Uber's pricing engine. No human reviews it. No one decides whether this is the right moment to surge. The algorithm reads the forecast, compares demand to available supply, calculates a multiplier, and pushes it to every rider's screen in milliseconds — the same logic firing for a concert crowd at 10pm and for an earthquake evacuation at 2am.

When the model is right, this automation is exactly what it should be: fast, consistent, and invisible. When the model is wrong — or when a situation it was never designed for arrives — the algorithm keeps firing anyway. The concert scenario works because the model was built for it. The earthquake scenario breaks because the algorithm cannot ask why demand spiked before deciding what to do about it. The model produces a number. The algorithm acts on it. The gap between those two steps is where human judgment either exists, or doesn't.

The prediction–decision gap

A forecast is not a decision — it is an input to one. Uber's system collapses that distinction by design. Understanding where automation is appropriate and where it requires a human in the loop is not a technical question. It is a governance question.

06 —

When the
Model Breaks

Six weeks after the concert, the world the model was trained on quietly stopped existing. WWU announced a 12% drop in fall enrollment — fewer students meant quieter Friday nights. A new apartment complex opened on Bellingham's north side, shifting where riders were actually requesting from. And Lyft launched in Whatcom County, pulling roughly 15% of the regular driver pool off the Uber platform. The model had no features for any of this. It was still predicting Friday nights as if it were January 2023 — because that is the world it learned from, and nothing had told it otherwise.

This is distribution shift: not a broken model, but a model predicting a world that no longer exists. For drivers, it meant surge pricing fired on nights that did not feel busy — because the algorithm was reading a supply gap caused by driver attrition and treating it as excess demand — so drivers chased phantom hotspots that were not hot anymore. For riders, it meant longer wait times and unexplained price spikes on ordinary Fridays, with no indication the forecast underlying those prices had gone stale. The error multiplier in the Lyft scenario reached 49.5× before the monitoring alert fired — nine full weeks of quietly wrong predictions, nine weeks of automated decisions built on a foundation that had already shifted.

07 —

My
Analysis

The following is my reflection on what this lab revealed about how AI systems actually work in practice — not just technically, but organizationally and ethically.

01
The most surprising thing to me was not the model itself, but how much the entire system depends on assumptions staying true. The model worked really well in backtesting and even held up on concert night, which gave a lot of confidence. But once the real world changed — like Lyft entering the market — the model didn't break loudly. It just quietly drifted and kept making confident predictions that were no longer true. That made me realize AI systems don't fail like code errors. They fail silently, and that is much more dangerous.
02
That ties directly into model reliability over time. Passing a backtest does not mean the model is "done" or safe to rely on — it just means it worked on the past. What I saw is that the model can be 95% accurate for months and then suddenly become wrong because the environment changes. The most important thing is not just building a good model, but constantly monitoring it and being ready to question it when the world shifts.
03
The earthquake scenario also really changed how I think about automation. My surge recommendation was technically correct based on the data, but clearly wrong in terms of impact. That showed me there is a line where algorithms should not make decisions alone. If the situation involves safety, emergencies, or human well-being, the system needs a checkpoint where a human can step in. The model can tell us what is happening, but it cannot understand why it is happening.
04
Playing the role of a driver was probably the most eye-opening perspective for me. As an analyst, everything looks clean and logical. But as a driver, I felt the cost of being wrong. Following the algorithm meant wasting time, gas, and income when the signal was off. That made me realize that AI decisions are not just numbers — they directly affect people's behavior and trust. If drivers stop trusting the system, they stop following it, and that alone can break the entire model.
05
If I were the data scientist behind this model, I would feel responsible for making sure it does not just work initially, but continues to work in the real world. But that responsibility should not sit only with the data team. Operations, product, and leadership all share it — because they are the ones using the model to make decisions that impact real people. AI in business is not just about building the model. It is about owning the outcomes it creates.
