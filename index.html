01 / Why forecast rides?

Getting the balance right matters

Enough cars.
In the right place.

If more people need rides than there are nearby drivers, waits grow and prices can rise. If the estimate is off, riders and drivers feel it. A forecast gives operations a starting point for planning busy nights. It is useful, but it is still only an estimate.

02 / A simple model

From past Fridays to a useful estimate

I created two years of fictional Friday night trip patterns near Western Washington University. Then I tested whether a basic model could follow the trend.

01

Build a sample

104 simulated weeks from 2023 through 2024, with seasonal and school year patterns.

02

Look for signals

Trend, summer, school in session and a New Year's Eve spike.

03

Test on later weeks

Train on the first 78 weeks. Hold the final 26 back for a fair check.

04 / A closer look

What changed the plan most?

I changed one assumption at a time to find where the recommendation was most sensitive.

The biggest lever

How many trips can one driver complete?

Slower pickups and concert traffic reduce trips per driver. In the model, lowering driver throughput toward 1.5 trips per hour increased the number of drivers needed. Adding drivers alone did less to change the shortage.

What the test showed

Surge stayed on in every scenario

The recommendation did not switch off, even in the more conservative cases. That makes it stable within this test, while also showing the limits of the assumptions behind it.

05 / Six weeks later

The city changed. The model did not.

In the project scenario, three shifts appeared after the concert. The model had no signal for any of them.

Three things moved
  • WWU enrollment fell by 12 percent.
  • A new apartment area shifted where rides began.
  • Lyft drew about 15 percent of regular drivers away.

The model kept using the old pattern. Its error grew quietly before monitoring noticed.

06 / What I learned

Good predictions still need good judgment

This project changed how I think about models in the real world.

1

Models can fail quietly

They may keep sounding confident after the world has changed.

2

Keep checking the fit

A good backtest describes the past. Monitoring helps spot new patterns.

3

Context matters

A concert and an emergency can create similar demand, but need different responses.

4

People feel the errors

Drivers can lose time and income when a signal sends them the wrong way.

5

Own the outcomes together

Data, operations and product teams all shape what happens after a forecast.
